# Startups keep the sprint, drop the ritual

Most startups do run sprints, but rarely by-the-book Scrum. The typical startup keeps a short **one-to-two-week cadence** (often renamed a "cycle"), a lightweight sync and a demo or review. It drops story points, velocity tracking, dedicated Scrum Masters and heavy ceremonies. Teams that leave sprints entirely usually go to one of two places. Interrupt-heavy teams move to **Kanban or continuous flow**. Teams making larger feature bets move to **Basecamp's Shape Up, with six-week cycles**. AI labs and AI-native startups (OpenAI, Anthropic, Cursor, Perplexity, Replit) sit further from Scrum still. They describe **bottom-up prototyping instead of roadmaps or PRDs**, small teams forming around prototypes that work, heavy dogfooding and continuous shipping. Anthropic's Claude Code team reportedly makes 60–100 internal releases a day. When these companies say "sprint", they mean a deadline-driven push, such as OpenAI's seven-week build of Codex, not a Scrum timebox. Survey data backs the broad picture: agile is near-universal, but **74% of State of Agile respondents now use hybrid, blended or homegrown models**. The evidence has real limits. No survey segments sprint use by startup stage. Page fetches were blocked during the research, so many claims below rest on search-engine snippets rather than full reads. Primary statements from AI labs about *research* (rather than product) planning were not found at all.

## Agile is near-universal, but textbook Scrum is the exception

The large surveys say agile is everywhere. Digital.ai's 17th State of Agile report (January 2024) headlined that **71% use agile in their software development lifecycle** ([official press release, Business Wire](https://businesswire.com/news/home/20240116199385/en/17th-State-of-Agile-Report-71-Use-Agile-in-their-SDLC-Small-Organizations-Report-Strong-Business-Benefits-Medium-and-Larger-Sized-Companies-Continue-to-Experience-Barriers-in-Successfully-Scaling-Agile)). The 18th report (October 2025) found that **74% now run "hybrid, blended, or homegrown" models** rather than a pure framework ([official press release, Digital.ai](https://digital.ai/press-releases/digital-ais-18th-state-of-agile-report-marks-the-start-of-the-fourth-wave-of-software-delivery/); [official report PDF](https://info.digital.ai/rs/981-LQX-968/images/Digital-ai-18th-State-of-Agile.pdf)). That report's sample is only about 350 respondents, mostly coaches and consultants at large enterprises, so it describes the enterprise world better than the startup one. Secondary blogs disagree on Scrum's share, with figures ranging from 66% to 87% of agile teams, because they count "Scrum" and "Scrum hybrids" differently ([Parabol, company blog](https://www.parabol.co/blog/how-many-companies-use-scrum/); [Echometer, vendor blog](https://echometerapp.com/en/scrum-statistics-2026/)). Treat any single Scrum percentage with caution.

Tooling data is the best available proxy for sprint use. In the 2024 Stack Overflow survey, **Jira was used by 57.5% of respondents**, up from 52.4% in 2023 ([official survey, Stack Overflow](https://survey.stackoverflow.co/2024/); [getint.io, vendor blog summary](https://www.getint.io/blog/stack-overflow-developer-survey-2024-jira-leads-the-way)). Among the Pragmatic Engineer's startup-skewed readership, Jira and Linear together accounted for close to 75% of project-management tool mentions in 2025 ([Pragmatic Engineer, newsletter](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/the-pragmatic-engineer-2025-survey-part-2)). But having a sprint-capable tool does not prove a team runs Scrum, since many teams run Kanban boards inside Jira.

The most direct evidence on venture-backed companies is older. In Gergely Orosz's 2021 survey of more than 100 engineers, **"no formal methodology" or a simple plan-build-ship loop was the most common approach at public and VC-funded tech companies**. Engineers led projects and teams chose their own process. Scrum was more common at "large, non-tech companies, non-venture funded companies and consultancies". Within tech companies it appeared mainly on platform, security and infrastructure teams buried in inbound requests ([Pragmatic Engineer, newsletter, 2021](https://blog.pragmaticengineer.com/project-management-at-big-tech/)). Practitioner forums say the same thing. One commenter in a 2026 Reddit thread says most teams run a "scrum inspired custom tailored process" rather than real Scrum ([Reddit, via mirror](https://reddit.sentinel-team.org/posts/1rdpw0g/snapshots/2026-02-26T07%3A51%3A49.201817Z)). That matches the 74% hybrid figure.

## Startups run short "agile-ish" cycles stripped of ceremony

The canonical startup advice is a lightweight cycle built around one goal, not Scrum. Y Combinator's Michael Seibel describes the **two-week product cycle** he used at Socialcam ([YC, official blog, 2016](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/product-development-cycle-fundamentals)). Each cycle opened with a product meeting that picked one focus goal, such as new users or retention. The team sorted ideas by difficulty and impact and wrote them up as specs. Engineers then built without interruption, and the last three days were kept for testing. A summary of YC's Startup School talk for technical founders gives the same advice: take an "agile-ish" approach and evolve process as complexity grows ([summify.io, secondary video summary](https://summify.io/discover/tips-for-technical-startup-founders-startup-school-rP7bpY)). YC's broader instruction is to "write code and talk to users" ([YC, official blog](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/ycs-essential-startup-advice/)).

Tools now build this lighter sprint in. Atlassian still recommends **"a fixed two-week duration"** for teams new to sprints ([Atlassian, official docs](https://support.atlassian.com/jira-software-cloud/docs/what-is-a-sprint)). Linear, the tool most associated with startups, replaces sprints with **"cycles"**. Its documentation describes them as "similar to commonly used agile-flavored sprints", lasting 1–8 weeks. Cycles are "more lightweight than sprints because they don't tie in release goals". Unfinished issues roll forward automatically instead of counting as failures, and estimates are optional ([Linear, official docs](https://linear.app/docs/use-cycles.md); [Linear Method, official docs](https://linear.app/docs/linear-method)). Linear also runs itself with very little process. Founders set 12-month direction and the company does not use OKRs ([Lenny's Newsletter, via mirror](https://personal-learnings.pages.dev/reading_room/issues/lennys_newsletter/0326_how-linear-builds-product.html)). A 2024 podcast with its first engineering manager is titled "Linear: move fast with little process" ([Pragmatic Engineer, podcast](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/linear-move-fast-with-little-process)). Startups such as Plus write openly about running engineering "cycles" ([Plus, company blog](https://www.plusdocs.com/blog/how-we-use-engineering-cycles)).

Story points get cut first. Doctolib published "Why Scrum and Story Points Are Not for Everyone" ([Doctolib Engineering, Medium](https://medium.com/doctolib/why-scrum-and-story-points-are-not-for-everyone-ff0ecb7ddd64)). Wealize explained why it "ditched story points to be more feature-oriented" ([dev.to, company blog](https://dev.to/wealize/why-we-ditched-story-points-to-be-more-feature-oriented-3i1i)). The consultancy thoughtbot notes that points are "not a requirement to effectively prioritize features" ([thoughtbot, consultancy blog](https://thoughtbot.com/blog/the-real-story-behind-story-points.md)). The daily standup survives, but often moves into Slack. The bot vendor Geekbot claims customers including Zapier and a GitHub team, and says async standups take "5 mins or less" ([Geekbot, vendor customer story](https://geekbot.com/customer-story/zapier-keeps-the-work-flowing-with-geekbots-asynchronous-stand-ups/)). That is a vendor claim, not independent evidence.

Practitioners explain the trimming with one argument: ceremony overhead grows faster than its value on small teams whose priorities keep shifting. One dev.to author argues that two-week sprint planning is wasted when a founder can change priorities mid-day ([dev.to, practitioner blog](https://dev.to/michaeljauk/does-scrum-even-make-sense-for-small-startup-teams-388c)). Another sums it up as "agile works for startups, agile ceremonies don't" ([dev.to, practitioner blog](https://dev.to/khalfankm7/agile-works-for-startups-agile-ceremonies-dont-heres-the-difference-5b00)). A Reddit commenter who keeps dailies and refinement but has dropped sprints puts it more bluntly: "Sprints are garbage" ([Reddit, via mirror](https://reddit.sentinel-team.org/posts/1rq16fl/snapshots/2026-03-11T15%3A10%3A00.908203Z)). Orosz describes his Skype team doing the same thing. It "stopped caring about sprints, and dropped most rituals" once it shipped more often than the sprint length ([Pragmatic Engineer, newsletter](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/project-management-in-tech)).

Sprints have defenders too, and their case is fair. Other Reddit commenters say Scrum worked well for an autonomous small product team and encourages collaboration ([Reddit, via mirror](https://reddit.sentinel-team.org/posts/1s87p0k/snapshots/2026-03-31T05%3A04%3A24.228137Z)). An older Hacker News anecdote describes Scrum rescuing a dysfunctional engineering team at a 100-person startup ([Hacker News, forum, 2012](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=4510943)). The timebox also forces regular reflection points that badly run Kanban can skip ([ProjectManagement.com, industry blog](https://www.projectmanagement.com/blog-post/80383/scrum-vs-kanban--transition-from-iterative-sprint-increments-to-a-continuous-flow-model)). The fair reading is that the sprint's main surviving value is a **shared rhythm and a predictable stakeholder checkpoint**, not velocity measurement.

## Teams that quit sprints choose Kanban for interrupts or six-week bets for features

Companies that drop sprints entirely mostly go one of two ways, and the type of work decides which.

| Alternative | Cadence | Best fit | Named adopters | Reported failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Kanban / continuous flow | None fixed; WIP limits, periodic reviews | Interrupt-heavy, reactive work | Spendesk, La Redoute, Skim Technologies | Losing track of progress; drift to low-value work |
| Shape Up | 6-week build + 2-week cooldown | Larger, plannable feature bets | Parabol, Bellroy, Mixpanel, Pennylane, Beam Dental, Babylist, GoSee Travel | Cooldown eaten by spillover; poor fit for reactive work |
| Lightweight cycles | 1–2 weeks, no release goal | Small product teams wanting rhythm | Linear, Plus | Critics call it "sprints renamed" |
| Specs + demos | Spec, build, Friday demo | AI-assisted generalist engineers | Abnormal AI | Too new for outcome data |

**Kanban is the most common destination**, and teams usually choose it to absorb interruptions. Spendesk, a French spend-management scale-up, moved from Scrum to Kanban and reported a steady pipeline and easier handling of urgent work. It also admitted downsides: losing track of progress and drifting toward low-value work, which it countered with regular reviews ([Spendesk Engineering, company blog](https://engineering.spendesk.com/posts/our-migration-from-sprints-to-kanban/)). One small team replaced sprints with a five-column board and a **strict WIP limit of two items per developer** ([besthub.dev, practitioner blog](https://besthub.dev/articles/how-dropping-story-points-and-sprints-supercharged-our-team-s-productivity-547cd14f5109)). Pure Kanban is rare, though. A literature-review summary finds that most agile transitions move toward Kanban *or hybrid flow*, keeping selected Scrum elements ([ICSOFT, academic conference summary](https://www.icsoft.org/article/literature-review-finds-most-agile-transitions-move-beyond-scrum-toward-kanban-or-hybrid-flow.html)). The result is often called "Scrumban" ([Atlassian Community, community post](https://community.atlassian.com/forums/App-Central-articles/How-Scrum-became-a-burden-and-what-we-did-about-it/ba-p/3133342)).

**Shape Up is the main alternative for feature work.** Teams size shaped "pitches" by *appetite*, meaning how much time the work is worth, rather than by estimate. A "betting table" commits them to a **six-week build cycle**. A "circuit breaker" cancels unfinished work instead of extending it, and a **two-week cooldown** follows each cycle ([Basecamp, official book](https://basecamp.com/shapeup)). Its creator, Ryan Singer, pitches it as an escape from "endless sprints" ([Lenny's Podcast, podcast listing](https://www.audioscrape.com/podcast/lennys-podcast-product-career-growth/episode/a-better-way-to-plan-build-and-ship-products-ryan-singer-creator-of-shape-up-early-employee-at-37signals-32460)). Adopters report qualitative gains. Bellroy says it delivered more projects on time ([Bellroy, company engineering blog](https://exploring-better-ways.bellroy.com/using-shape-up-what-works-what-weve-changed-whats-next.html)). Mixpanel uses shaping and the circuit breaker to speed time to market ([Mixpanel, company blog via Plushcap](https://www.plushcap.com/content/mixpanel/blog/mixpanel-how-we-use-shaping-to-accelerate-time-to-market)). Parabol was on its 15th six-week cycle more than two years after leaving two-week sprints ([Parabol, company blog](https://www.parabol.co/?p=14541)). Coda left two-week sprints that "started to feel a little frantic" for six-week cycles under half-year planning ([Lenny's Newsletter, via mirror](https://personal-learnings.pages.dev/reading_room/issues/lennys_newsletter/0414_how-coda-builds-product.html)).

Shape Up is not a clean win. Adopters such as Slite and Memo Bank reportedly stretched cycles to eight weeks ([fmerian, practitioner blog on dev.to](https://dev.to/fmerian/experimenting-with-shape-up-3dnj)). One practitioner found the cooldown "turned into not much of a cool-down" as spillover piled in ([Alex Debecker, Substack](https://alexdebecker.substack.com/p/the-uncomfortable-shape-up-cool-down)). Scalex **switched back after two years**, because reactive work made six-week cycles hard to fill ([Scalex, company blog](https://scalex.dev/blog/2-years-with-shape-up/)). No adopter published before-and-after metrics, so the claimed gains are self-reported.

## AI labs ship around prototypes, not sprint backlogs

No source found says a frontier lab or AI-native startup runs textbook Scrum, and none explicitly rules it out either. What they describe publicly is a different operating model. Calvin French-Owen, who worked at OpenAI from May 2024 to June 2025, says work there was **bottom-up**. Small groups built prototypes without approval, teams formed around the promising ones, and he saw three or four similar prototypes before Codex launched ([Xenospectrum, secondary news](https://xenospectrum.com/openais-chaotic-internal-situation-former-engineer-discusses-the-light-and-shadow-of-rapid-growth/)). When he asked early on about a quarterly roadmap, he was reportedly told none existed ([36Kr, secondary news](https://www.36kr.com/p/3381278242564609)). Codex itself came out of a **seven-week push** that merged two competing teams ([Analytics India Mag, secondary news](https://analyticsindiamag.com/ai-news-updates/openai-built-codex-in-just-7-weeks-from-scratch/)). His original essay at calv.info could not be fetched. ChatGPT engineering head Sulman Choudhry describes a "chaos factory" culture that runs several independent efforts before converging on one ([Engineering Leadership Podcast, episode summary](https://metacast.app/podcast/the-engineering-leadership-podcast/hereHPAV/how-openai-s-engineering-org-is-reshaping-teams-roles-and-workflows-w-sulman-choudhry-250/8JeXJ6ZI)).

Anthropic's Claude Code team takes the same approach. **Working prototypes replaced PRDs**: the team builds many versions before shipping a feature ([Pragmatic Engineer, podcast](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/building-claude-code-with-boris-cherny)). Boris Cherny is quoted as saying, "We don't write much. We just show" ([36Kr, secondary news](https://eu.36kr.com/en/p/3727086198963080)). A dogfooding-ready version shipped two months after the first prototype, and **half of Anthropic Engineering was using it by day five** ([Pragmatic Engineer, newsletter](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/how-claude-code-is-built)). A secondary summary reports **60–100 internal releases a day** ([Pixel Pioneers, secondary blog](https://thepixelpioneers.blogspot.com/2025/09/how-claude-code-is-built.html)). Anthropic's own post shows teams setting up autonomous loops in which Claude Code writes code, runs tests and iterates while humans review ([Anthropic, official blog](https://claude.com/blog/how-anthropic-teams-use-claude-code)). This was the only primary page actually read in full during the research. One unofficial summary says the product team does delegate *draft PRDs* to Claude, which complicates the "no PRDs" story ([PM archive, secondary summary](https://the-pm-archive.vercel.app/articles/2026-04-28-how-anthropics-product-team-ships-in-days-not-months-cat-wus-playbook-for-pms-in-the-ai-era)).

Other AI startups describe similar models. A Cursor job posting says the company has **"no roadmap committee"**, relies on product-minded engineers, and prefers shipping "the high-leverage thing now" ([Anysphere, official careers page](https://anysphere.inc/careers/enterprise_product_engineer)). Separate PM listings show the PM role still exists ([ResumeGeni, job aggregator](https://resumegeni.com/jobs/product-manager-at-anysphere-san-francisco)). Perplexity's Aravind Srinivas reportedly ships products about 80% complete and iterates ([Particle News, secondary](https://particlenews.pages.dev/story/perplexity-ceo-aravind-srinivas-outlines-80-then-iterate-strategy-at-uc-berkeley-haas)). He calls speed "your only strategy… like running a marathon in full sprint", which uses "sprint" as a metaphor, not a method ([dev.to, secondary](https://dev.to/maybe_ai/the-survival-code-of-perplexitys-ceo-how-to-break-through-the-siege-of-ai-giants-1g60)). Replit's Amjad Masad described a **nightly agent that reads user traces, opens PRs with prompt changes and ships them as A/B tests**, so part of the improvement loop runs without any human schedule ([SaaStr, conference write-up](https://www.saastr.com/amjad-masad-and-me-at-saastr-ai-2026-the-agents-we-actually-built-and-what-replits-founder-thinks-comes-next/)).

Research work fits sprints worse than product engineering does. Practitioners note that ML tasks are hard to estimate and that **training runs take days or weeks**. The output is often insight rather than a shippable increment. One author compares Scrum on data science to making university researchers "switch their research subject at random every two weeks" ([Amethix, Medium](https://medium.com/amethix/why-is-scrum-a-terrible-idea-for-machine-learning-2325826f3104); [IIA, industry blog](https://iianalytics.com/community/blog/dont-make-data-scientists-do-scrum)). A LinkedIn article (read via a mirror) makes the same point about training runs derailing sprints ([LinkedIn, via mirror](https://meilu1.jpshuntong.com/url-68747470733a2f2f7777772e6c696e6b6564696e2e636f6d/pulse/why-scrum-struggles-ai-projects-rethinking-agile-machine-alex-alieja-3p8cf)). In a small Data Science Process Alliance poll (n=109), **CRISP-DM led at about 49%, ahead of Scrum at about 18% and Kanban at about 12%** ([Data Science PM, practitioner org](https://www.datascience-pm.com/crisp-dm-still-most-popular/)). A 2022 academic survey found that only 25% of participants used any formal data-science methodology ([arXiv, academic paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.06310)). How frontier labs plan pretraining and RL research is still unknown. Milestone-, compute- and eval-driven planning is the likely answer, but no lab source confirms it.

## AI coding agents are squeezing the two-week sprint

A 2025–26 wave of commentary argues that AI agents make the two-week sprint too slow. When an agent can build a feature before planning ends, the bottleneck moves from writing code to **specifying intent and reviewing output** ([HackerNoon, community blog](https://hackernoon.com/ai-coding-agents-are-breaking-the-two-week-sprint); [Invide Labs, company blog via HN reader](https://hn.jakechampion.name/reader/https://blog.invidelabs.com/the-death-of-the-two-week-sprint)). Abnormal AI's engineering team found that two-week sprints created more planning overhead than generalist engineers shipping end to end in days needed. It piloted **"specs, not sprints"** with Friday demos ([Abnormal AI, company engineering blog](https://builders.abnormal.ai/p/specs-not-sprints)). OpenAI's "harness engineering" post describes a team that shipped an internal beta over five months with **"0 lines of manually-written code"**, recasting engineers as designers of the agents' environment and feedback loops ([OpenAI, official blog](https://openai.com/index/harness-engineering/)). Dataconomy goes the other way and calls the trend "Waterfall 2.0". In its view, spec-first development returns, and sprint planning shrinks to approving AI-generated task breakdowns ([Dataconomy, trade press](https://dataconomy.com/2025/11/14/waterfall-2-0-ai-brings-back-structured-software-development)).

The other side has a case too. A talk at AI Engineer Code 2025 noted that **most organisations still run 8–10-person teams on two-week sprints** ([AI Engineer, conference talk](https://ai.engineer/talks/SZStlIhyTCY-moving-away-from-agile-whats-next)). StickyMinds argues agile is "far from dead" ([StickyMinds, trade press](https://www.stickyminds.com/article/agile-dead-how-ai-coding-transforming-software-development)). Digital.ai found AI use in agile delivery rising **from 68% to 84%**, inside agile processes rather than replacing them ([Digital.ai, official press release](https://digital.ai/press-releases/digital-ais-18th-state-of-agile-report-marks-the-start-of-the-fourth-wave-of-software-delivery/)). This echoes Agile Manifesto signatory Dave Thomas's older distinction: the branded noun "Agile" can die while *agility* lives on ([InfoQ, tech news, 2014](https://www.infoq.com/news/2014/10/pragmatic-dave-agility)). The most likely outcome is that the sprint stays as a coordination and stakeholder cadence while delivery inside it becomes continuous. No quantitative study yet measures sprint-length change at AI-adopting companies.

## The evidence rests on snippets, vendors and dated surveys

The user asked for sources across blogs, Reddit, YouTube, LinkedIn, Medium and official company sites. Coverage of those source types was uneven, and the access limits shape how much weight the findings can bear. **WebFetch was blocked on nearly every host.** The only primary page read in full was Anthropic's Claude blog. Everything else comes from search-result snippets and summaries, so exact quotes and figures should be checked before reuse.

| Source type | Coverage | Caveat |
|---|---|---|
| Official company sites/docs | Strong (YC, Atlassian, Linear, Basecamp, OpenAI, Anthropic, Digital.ai, Anysphere) | Mostly snippets; vendor docs describe intended, not observed, use |
| Company engineering blogs (incl. Medium) | Strong (Spendesk, Doctolib, Pennylane, Beam Dental, Bellroy, Parabol, Scalex, Abnormal) | Self-reported, no before/after metrics |
| Newsletters/podcasts | Strong (Pragmatic Engineer, Lenny's) | Paywalled or read via mirrors; key 2021 survey is dated |
| Reddit | Weak | Read via mirror sites; subreddits unconfirmed |
| LinkedIn | Very weak | One article via a mirror; no verifiable posts |
| YouTube | Very weak | Relevant videos surfaced only as podcast listings or summaries; e.g. a "Big Tech No Scrum" video ([YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VXwIW4DSUhA)) was not reviewed |
| Secondary news on AI labs | Moderate | Many OpenAI details come via translated Chinese/Japanese coverage of one essay |

Some widely repeated figures could not be verified. One example is a claim that "59% of teams still run two-week sprints, declining every year since 2022", attributed to State of Agile but found only in low-authority blogs ([dev.to, community blog](https://dev.to/8080_ai/how-multi-agent-ai-platforms-are-changing-the-software-development-sprint-2cae)). Nothing in the notes segments sprint use by seed, Series A or growth stage. There are no primary sources on how Google DeepMind, Mistral, Stripe, Vercel or Figma plan their work.

## Conclusion

The question "do startups use sprints?" sets up a false choice. The real variable is which parts of Scrum a team keeps. Startups keep the **timebox as a rhythm** and drop the parts that assume stable priorities and known business value: estimation, velocity and committed scope. That is why Linear's cycles, which roll unfinished work forward and set no release goal, have become the startup default rather than formal Scrum or no cadence at all. The choice between Kanban and Shape Up is best made by type of work, not by philosophy. Interrupt-driven teams need flow. Teams making bets need a protected appetite. Shape Up's frequent stretching to eight weeks, and Scalex's reversal, show that neither choice is permanent.

AI companies show where this is heading. When building is cheap, **the working prototype becomes the spec and the planning ritual loses value**. The scarce resources become review capacity, evaluation and clarity of intent. That suggests the next process innovations will look less like new sprint formats and more like ways to manage review queues and agent feedback loops. Replit's nightly agent and OpenAI's harness engineering are early examples. For a startup choosing a process today, a practical default follows from the evidence. Run a short cycle for alignment, skip story points, put WIP limits on review, and hold demos often enough that stakeholders see working software rather than burndown charts.
